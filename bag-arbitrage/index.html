Cross-Border Luxury Pricing

Official list prices for the same products in the United States and Canada

Key findings Code Report
Abstract

I compare official list prices for matched luxury products on brands' US and Canadian websites. Results are loaded from results.json, the output of the analysis pipeline.

Research question

Is the same product priced differently across the border?

For identical products sold on a brand's official US and Canadian websites, how do Canadian list prices compare with US list prices converted at the market exchange rate?

Unit and population

The unit is a product listing: one style or reference code as sold in both markets, so two colours of one bag count as two listings. A model-level check (one row per identical price pair) gives almost the same answer; see Statistical evidence. The population is listings the selected brands show with a price on both official sites. Brands and products were chosen, not sampled at random, so results describe this sample.

Estimand

di = ln( PCAi ⁄ (X · PUSi) )

Prices before sales tax; X is the Bank of Canada CAD-per-USD rate on the last business day before collection. The target is the mean of di; d < 0 means cheaper in Canada.

Hypotheses and inference

H₀: mean d = 0 against a two-sided alternative. Products from one brand are priced together, so standard errors are clustered by brand and the t reference has one degree of freedom per brand, minus one.

Methods

From product pages to an estimate

Each step is a script in the pipeline. Click a step for details; the report has the full methods.

  1. Official list prices, both markets, one day.

    Each product was opened on the brand's official US and Canadian sites on 13 October 2026. The list price, currency, style code, source URL and a provenance note were recorded in collected_prices.csv. Source links show each page as it was on that day. Brands often remove or move product pages, so some links may stop working later; the results use only the recorded prices and do not depend on the links. Internet Archive copies, where the archive could make one, are listed with their capture dates in archived_sources.csv. A copy captured on another day may show a different price.

  2. Format checks and matching flags.

    validate.py rejects wrong currencies and non-positive prices, reports unmatched products, and flags name-based matches, listing-page prices, sold-out items and repeated price pairs. Flagged rows stay in the sample and get their own sensitivity analysis.

  3. Log difference at a dated exchange rate.

    d = ln(CA price ÷ (US price × rate)), using the Bank of Canada FXUSDCAD rate for 9 October 2026. Tax and other rates are scenarios: each shifts every d by the same constant.

  4. Mean d with brand-clustered inference.

    CR1 cluster-robust standard error by brand with t(G − 1) critical values, a wild cluster bootstrap-t, and a regression of d on log price and category, all in bastats.py.

  5. Robustness, influence, tests.

    Alternative weightings and samples, leave-one-brand-out, and 26 unit tests, mostly on synthetic data with known answers (tests/).

Limitations

What this study cannot tell you

The result is a careful description of one sample on one day. These are the main reasons to be cautious.

L1 · Design

Descriptive, not causal

The data show that Canadian list prices are lower after conversion. They cannot show why: infrequent repricing after exchange-rate moves, taxes and duties, competition or deliberate pricing to market are all possible and none is tested here.

L2 · Selection

A chosen, not random, sample

Brands were included only if both official sites showed prices. Many publish none online or blocked access (for example Chanel, Louis Vuitton, Gucci, Cartier, Patek Philippe, Audemars Piguet). About ten well-known products per brand were chosen by hand, so results may not extend to other brands or products.

L3 · Price definition

List prices, not what buyers pay

Sales, waiting lists, secondary-market premiums, duty on cross-border purchases and tourist tax refunds are not observed. Popular Rolex models in particular trade above list price.

L4 · Timing

A one-day snapshot

All prices were read on one day. Because list prices change less often than the exchange rate, the measured gap would differ on another date.

L5 · Inference

Fifteen brands

Brand-clustered inference rests on 15 clusters, which is few. Brand-level and subset results are imprecise; small leather goods come from only three brands.

L6 · Matching and sources

Some matches use names

Where a brand shows no style code on its listing, US and Canadian prices were matched by exact product name, and some prices were read from category listing pages rather than product pages. Each product in the explorer says which. Excluding either group is one of the robustness checks.

L7 · Sources

Brand websites only

Third-party sources were checked and not used: Hudson's Bay has closed, Saks Canada disallows crawlers, Farfetch and NET-A-PORTER show retailer prices, and Chrono24 is mostly resale listings.
