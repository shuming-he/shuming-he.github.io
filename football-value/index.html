Market Value vs On-Field Performance

How much of a footballer’s Transfermarkt value do league statistics explain? Premier League, LaLiga and Bundesliga, 2025/26

Author

Shuming He

Published

October 14, 2026

A season of league statistics, together with age, position, league and contract length, predicts most of a footballer’s Transfermarkt value. The statistics on their own explain only about a quarter of it, and the same numbers are worth far more in the Premier League.

Key findings

  1. Most of a player’s value is predictable from a few simple facts. For clubs the model never saw, it explains 83% of the variation in (log) market value. A typical prediction is still off by a factor of about 1.7, up or down.
  2. On-field numbers matter, but they are not most of the story. League statistics alone explain 26%, and about 33% of what the full model explains. Age, position, league and contract do the rest.
  3. Playing time, goals and team success count most. Playing 10 percentage points more of the league’s minutes goes with about +15% value, 0.1 more goals per 90 minutes with about +13%, and a club that earns 0.5 more points per game with about +72%. Yellow cards show no clear link.
  4. The same numbers are priced very differently across leagues. With identical statistics, age, position and contract, LaLiga players are valued about −65% and Bundesliga players about −63% relative to Premier League players.
  5. The leagues may also reward different things, but the evidence is only moderate. The test that all three leagues reward the statistics equally gives p = 0.047 with the more cautious bootstrap method.
  6. A big gap between actual and predicted value is not a mistake by the market. It mostly reflects what this model cannot see: defending, passing, injuries, earlier seasons and reputation.

The sections below give the details, the uncertainty around each number and the checks behind them.

Question

Transfermarkt publishes a market value for every professional footballer. Those values are set by its community and editors, not by a formula, and they are widely quoted in transfer reporting. This project asks three things:

  1. How much of a player’s market value can be explained by what he does on the pitch in league matches?
  2. Which players are valued well above or below what those statistics predict?
  3. Do the Premier League, LaLiga and Bundesliga reward the same statistics in the same way?

The analysis is descriptive. A player whose value is above the prediction is not shown to be “overvalued”: his value may reflect things the statistics here do not measure, such as defensive work, passing, injuries, earlier seasons, international reputation or commercial appeal.

Data

  • Source. transfermarkt-datasets, a public dataset built from Transfermarkt pages and released under CC0. It was downloaded on 14 October 2026; PROVENANCE.md records the file’s SHA-256 hash and the rows kept.
  • Why not scrape? Transfermarkt’s terms do not allow automated collection. FBref, the usual source for detailed match statistics, stopped publishing Opta’s advanced data in 2026, so passing and defensive statistics were not available from it either.
  • Unit. One outfield player in one league season. Statistics come from that season’s league matches only. A player who changed club within the league is counted once, at the club where he played most minutes.
  • Outcome. The latest market value Transfermarkt published between 1 May and 30 June 2026, after the season ended. The values range from €200k to €200.0m, with median €12.0m. The model uses the log of value, because values span three orders of magnitude.
  • Sample. At least 900 league minutes, goalkeepers excluded. That leaves 316 Premier League, 321 LaLiga and 265 Bundesliga players from 58 clubs.

Method

The primary model is an ordinary least squares regression of log market value on:

  • League statistics: share of the season’s league minutes played, goals per 90 minutes, assists per 90 minutes, yellow cards per 90 minutes, and the club’s points per game.
  • Other factors: league, position group (seven groups), age (a natural cubic spline with 4 degrees of freedom), years left on the contract and a flag for a missing contract date.

Players at the same club share a coach, a team style and a market profile, so their errors are correlated. Standard errors are therefore clustered by club (58 clusters, t with 57 degrees of freedom). For the main league comparison I also report a wild cluster bootstrap with 1999 draws, which is more reliable when a test uses many restrictions relative to the number of clusters.

To answer “how much is explained” without rewarding overfitting, R² is also computed out of sample. Clubs are split into 10 folds, and each club’s players are predicted by a model that never saw that club.

How much do statistics explain?

Model In-sample R² Out-of-sample R²
League statistics only – 0.26
League statistics + league 0.52 0.49
Age, position, league, contract only 0.58 0.54
All of the above 0.85 0.83

The league statistics are jointly significant (F(5, 57) = 79.6, p = 2.0 × 10−24). Splitting the out-of-sample R² of the full model fairly between the two groups of variables (a two-group Shapley decomposition) gives league statistics 33% of the explained variation and the other factors the rest. After the model, the typical prediction error is a factor of about 1.66 in either direction (residual SD 0.51 in logs).

Do the leagues value players differently?

In level, yes, strongly. For the same statistics, age, position and contract, a LaLiga player’s value is −65% (95% CI −71% to −57%) and a Bundesliga player’s −63% (95% CI −69% to −56%) compared with a Premier League player. Part of this is the Premier League’s revenue, and part may be differences in opposition strength that per-90 numbers do not adjust for.

In what is rewarded, there is moderate evidence of differences. Letting all five statistics have their own slope in each league and testing that the slopes are equal gives F(10, 57) = 2.87, p = 0.006 with clustered standard errors, and p = 0.047 with the wild cluster bootstrap. The bootstrap p-value is much weaker, so the evidence is real but not strong. Separate regressions by league (coefficients on log value, clustered SE in brackets):

In the Premier League, value rises less steeply with club results, minutes, goals and assists than in the other two leagues. That pattern is consistent with Premier League values being high even at weaker clubs, but this analysis does not test the reason. Each league regression has only 18–20 clubs, so these individual slopes are imprecise.

Above and below what the statistics predict

The full table for all 902 players can be downloaded as residuals.csv. Each player’s prediction comes from the out-of-sample model, so his own club was not used to fit it. The ratio is actual value ÷ predicted value: 2.0 means valued at twice the prediction, 0.5 at half.

Read this before the table

A high ratio does not mean a player is overvalued, and a low ratio does not mean he is a bargain. The model only sees minutes, goals, assists, cards, club results, age, position, league and contract. Anything else that buyers pay for, such as defending, passing, injury history, earlier seasons or reputation, ends up in the ratio.

Two cautions before reading the list. First, a typical player is already a factor of 1.66 away from the prediction, so only large ratios stand out. Second, a large gap often points to something the model leaves out: age at the extremes (one season says little about a 38-year-old’s value), defensive and passing work these statistics do not record, or a club whose results were unusual that season.

Do these gaps last?

Using the same model in earlier seasons (without contract length, because the source only has current contract dates), the gap in one season is correlated with the same player’s gap the next season: r = 0.44 from 2023/24 to 2024/25 (n = 544) and r = 0.47 from 2024/25 to 2025/26 (n = 558). So much of the gap is a stable feature of the player that these statistics do not capture, not a one-season accident.

Players valued above their prediction also tended to see their value grow more slowly the next year: each 1.0 of log gap went with −0.16 (SE 0.03) and −0.24 (SE 0.03) in the next year’s log change, controlling for age. Two explanations fit this pattern. The market may gradually correct values that ran ahead of performance. Or it may be ordinary regression toward the mean: a gap measured with noise in one season tends to shrink in the next even when nothing is corrected. This data cannot tell the two apart, so the pattern is not evidence that the market was wrong.

Limitations

  • Missing statistics. Without passing, defensive or expected-goals data, the model cannot see much of what defenders and midfielders do. Their residuals mostly measure that gap.
  • Market value is an estimate. Transfermarkt values are community judgements. Research has found them to be informative about actual transfer fees (Herm, Callsen-Bracker and Kreis 2014; Müller, Simons and Weinmann 2017), but they are not prices.
  • One season, current positions. Position and contract dates in the source are the latest ones, not necessarily those during the season.
  • Association, not cause. Better players play more and score more, and also join better clubs. The coefficients describe how values line up with statistics, not what would happen to a value if a player scored more.
  • Selection. Players with fewer than 900 minutes are excluded, so the results describe regular players.

Reproduce

All code, data and this report are in the repository. From football-value/pipeline/:

pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m unittest discover tests
python run_analysis.py      # dataset, models, results.json, tables
python make_report.py       # writes ../index.qmd
quarto render ../index.qmd

The GitHub Action that publishes this page runs the tests and the analysis, and fails if the recomputed results.json differs from the committed one.
